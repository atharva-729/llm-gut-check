A personal experiment · the question

Can we trust an LLM's gut?

With people, intuition is trustworthy in "kind" settings, where patterns are regular and feedback is quick, and unreliable in "wicked" ones (Kahneman & Klein, 2009). So I wanted to test a tempting idea: when an LLM answers on instinct and says it's at least 90% sure, maybe it's right nearly every time, say 99%. If that were true, you could let the confident answers through and send only the unsure ones to a person.

When a model's quick answer says "≥ 90% sure", is it really right ~99% of the time?
Does making the model think before answering make it more trustworthy?
Does it depend on the task: kind (grammar) versus wicked (judging toxicity)?

Act 2 · The data

Three tasks, from kind to wicked

Each task has a known correct answer for every item, so grading is automatic and nobody hand-labels anything. They were picked to sit at different points on the kind–wicked scale.

How to read it: left is "kind": one right answer, regular rules. Right is "wicked": the "right" answer is a vote among people who often disagree. Banking sits in between, with clear intents but 77 of them, and many look alike.

Act 3 · What we tested

Gut versus thinking, three models, two rounds

We used the cheap tier from three vendors, since that's what high-volume work actually runs on: gpt-5.4-mini, Claude Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash. Every item was asked twice: once in gut mode (thinking switched off) and once in thinking mode (reason first). Each time, the model returned an answer and how sure it was.

How to read it: one "call" is one question to one model in one mode. Before starting we checked that gut mode really used zero thinking tokens for every model. Gemini 3.8 Flash couldn't fully switch thinking off, so we used Gemini 3.5 Flash instead.

How to read it: round 1 was for looking around, and that's where we spotted patterns. Then we wrote down exactly what we expected and saved it in git, time-stamped, before round 2 existed. Round 2 then tested those predictions on fresh items. That way a pattern found by luck can't pass as a result.

Act 5 · Conclusion
No. An LLM's gut can't be trusted on its own.
✕
✕Thinking first isn't a dependable fix. It helped on grammar, mostly did nothing on banking intents, and made one model worse at judging toxicity.
✕
✓The kind/wicked idea partly holds: the models' instincts were most reliable on grammar and least reliable on moderation, the same place human intuition struggles.
✓What does work is the method: test each task separately, set cut-offs on one batch, and check them on a fresh batch before removing the person.
Everything explained

The ideas behind the experiment, one at a time

Gut mode and thinking mode

Newer models can "reason" privately before answering, which takes more time and money. Each vendor has its own switch: reasoning_effort for OpenAI, extended thinking for Anthropic, a thinking budget for Google. "Gut" means that switch is off, and we confirmed it by checking that the usage report showed 0 thinking tokens.

Stated confidence

The model is asked to return {"answer": …, "confidence": 0–100}, where confidence is "the chance you're right". It's a number the model writes down, not a measured probability. That's exactly why it has to be checked.

Accuracy and its uncertainty range

With a limited number of items, any accuracy is an estimate. The range shows where the true value probably lies. More items, narrower range. That's why round 2 used 600 items per task, not 200.

Calibration

A model is calibrated if, among answers given at 80% confidence, about 80% are right. The reliability chart (Result 3) plots this. Points below the diagonal mean overconfidence.

Auto-accept cut-off, coverage, precision

A cut-off is a rule such as "auto-accept if confidence ≥ 95%". Coverage is the share of items it lets through without review. Precision is the share of those that are right. A higher cut-off usually raises precision and lowers coverage.

"Safe" and why 99% was out of reach

We called a cut-off safe only if even a pessimistic estimate of its precision (the Wilson lower bound) was ≥ 95%, with at least 30 items. A 99% target needs about correct answers in a row above the cut-off just to be provable. With 200 or 600 items per task that was never realistic.

Flips and overthinking

Compare each item's gut answer with its thinking answer. Fix = wrong → right. Overthinking = right → wrong. We flagged "overthinking risk" when breaks were at least 3% of items or at least 30% of all changes.

Pre-registration

Writing predictions and decision rules down before seeing the test data, and committing them to git so the timestamp proves it. It stops you from finding a pattern by chance and then telling a story about it afterwards.

Discovery versus confirmation

Round 1 (discovery) is where cut-offs were chosen and patterns spotted. Round 2 (confirmation) applied them unchanged to new items. Result 5 shows why this matters: many rules looked safe in round 1 and failed in round 2.

Review-cost model

Total cost per 1,000 items = AI calls + for each item a person reviews + for each wrong answer auto-accepted. These prices are assumptions, but the conclusion holds whatever you plug in, because the AI part is tiny either way.

Why these three tasks

Grammar (BLiMP) is the kind extreme. Banking intents (Banking77) is a realistic business routing task. Moderation (Civil Comments) is the wicked one: labels are crowd votes, so "truth" is itself uncertain.

Limits

  • Three cheap models, one prompt template, three public datasets that the models may have seen in training.
  • Confidence is the model's own stated number. Token-probability confidence wasn't tried.
  • Moderation "truth" is a crowd vote, so part of the error is disagreement about policy rather than model failure.
  • Review and error costs are assumptions. Gemini prices come from third-party listings.
  • The ≥ 90% check in Result 1 is exploratory. It directly tests the starting idea, but it wasn't among the locked predictions.
